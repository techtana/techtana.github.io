I once advised an engineering intern to start her internship by drafting the final presentation on her experience at Micron; from learning the domain fundamentals and having opportunities to explore other departments to creating a project that impact our customer and having travelled to Sawtooth Mountains. It sounds backward to start with a presentation, but it's the same way as letting her craft a personal narrative about the growth she wanted. Writing down her objectives and draw a picture of successful journey gave us a tangible anchor and milestones to look forward to, and forces clarity for how my team and I can support those goals.

That said, not everyone walks in with a crisp vision on day one - and that's okay. A draft can feel like pressure or premature certainty if you treat it like a contract. I frame it as a living document: a first swing, not a final verdict. Think of it as hypotheses we'll test together. Exploration matters; serendipity still gets a vote.

I learned this approach during my own internship, guided by a professor who told me: "Know why you started, know why you stayed, and know why you quit." Without clarity and reminders, it's easy to drift or make decisions based on temporary emotions. Reflection grounds choices in logic and long-term fit. But temporary emotions are still data. Sometimes the thing that frustrates you is the thing you shouldn't ignore. Balance the plan with how the work actually makes you feel.

An internship isn't just an extended interview for the company; it's also a chance for interns to assess the role, the work, the team, and even the city against their vision. And to be fair, it's a chance for the company to learn, too, about the problems we're giving interns, the support we provide, and whether our process makes room for people who are still figuring it out.

The draft presentation is more than a future deliverable. It reminds mentors and teammates that we share responsibility for creating meaningful opportunities where interns can prove themselves. It also encourages interns to show initiative - the values our team prizes. Just keep it lightweight: a one-pager or a scrappy slide or two beats a polished deck that steals time from real work. Don't confuse sleek slides with progress; use regular check-ins to revise the plan, celebrate pivots, and document lessons. If the draft evolves, that's not failure; that's growth doing its job.
